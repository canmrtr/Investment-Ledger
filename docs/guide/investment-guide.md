# My Investment Guide

> *"Investing has a price — not in dollars, but in volatility, fear, doubt, uncertainty and regret."*

This guide is built from my own notes and the frameworks I've developed over time. It is a practical reference, not a textbook.

---

## Part 1: Philosophy

**The market is managed by expectations.** Investing is about measuring business success, not predicting prices. The market is both efficient (math matters) and behavioral (psychology creates opportunity). When everyone is panicking, calm investors buy great assets at lower prices.

**The minimum bar:** If you cannot consistently return 12–15% per year picking your own stocks, buy an S&P 500 index fund. Stock picking has to beat the alternative to be worth the effort.

**Compounding is the engine.** $1 growing at 10% earns $1.59 in the first 10 years — and $8,468 in the last 10. The biggest gains come at the end of a long holding period. A fast compounder will eventually overtake a slow one even with a smaller starting base.

**Know your number.** Financial freedom means having enough capital to cover your recurring expenses through returns (ROI). Define your number first, then build toward it.

## Part 2: Before You Pick a Stock

### 2.1 Know Your Risk Profile

Your portfolio structure depends on two things:

| Factor | What to consider |
|--------|-----------------|
| **Ability to take risk** | Age, lifestyle, current assets, dependants |
| **Willingness to take risk** | Can you sleep at night with this allocation? |

- **Short time horizon** → liquid, less volatile investments
- **Long time horizon** → riskier investments with better long-term rewards

### 2.2 Tenbaggers Are for the Patient

A tenbagger (10x return) requires time. Younger investors have more time to search for them. Older investors should weight toward safer, more predictable positions. Adjust your expectations accordingly.

### 2.3 Avoid FOMO

Hot stocks and popular funds are dangerous entry points. Investing because everyone else is = unsuccessful investing. The crowd's enthusiasm is already priced in.

## Part 3: Finding the Right Company

### 3.1 Company Lifecycle — When to Buy

A company goes through three stages:

1. **Start-up** — high risk, establishing basics
2. **Rapid expansion** — the optimal entry point; best risk/reward ratio
3. **Maturity** — growth slows, limited upside

**The safest and most profitable time to invest is the rapid expansion phase.**

### 3.2 The 3 Pillars of a Quality Business

Every stock I consider must demonstrate:

1. **Strong, predictable cash generation** — FCF margin > 5%, operating margins sustainable
2. **Sustainably high returns on capital** — ROE > 15%, ROA > 7%, ROIC > 15%
3. **Attractive growth opportunities backed by a competitive moat**

### 3.3 The Moat

A moat is a sustainable competitive advantage. Look for:

- **Network effects** — the product becomes more valuable as more people use it
- **Switching costs** — customers can't easily leave
- **Cost advantages** — structurally lower costs than competitors
- **Learning curves** — expertise built over time that new entrants can't replicate

### 3.4 Industry Analysis

Before investing in a company, map its industry:

**Create an industry map:**
- List all players by size (incumbents + new entrants)
- Identify suppliers (cost of inputs) and customers (willingness to pay)
- Identify external factors (regulation, government action)
- Find where value is actually being created

**Assess industry stability:**
- Look for 5 years of market share data — lower change = more stable industry
- Stable pricing power is a strong signal of stability

**Five Forces (Porter):**

| Force | Key questions |
|-------|--------------|
| Supplier power | Switching costs? Substitute inputs? |
| Buyer power | Switching costs? Substitute products? |
| Substitution threat | Can the product be replaced? |
| Entrant threat | Asset specificity, learning curves, network effects as barriers |
| Competitive rivalry | How do existing players interact? |

**Profit Pool analysis:** Map ROIC (vertical) vs. investment share (horizontal) to see which segment of the industry captures the most value. Invest in companies that sit at the top of the profit pool.

## Part 4: Management Evaluation

A strong management team is non-negotiable.

| Signal | What it means |
|--------|--------------|
| **Owns significant stock** | Shareholder interests are their interests |
| **Long-term vision** | Invests in organic growth, not short-term games |
| **CEO is not a media celebrity** | Proceed with caution if the CEO is constantly in the press |
| **Insider purchases** | When insiders buy during a dip, it's a strong buy signal |

**Skin in the game:** If there is insider purchasing during a drawdown, that is an opportunity to buy at prices lower than what management paid.

## Part 5: Stock Evaluation — The 20-Criteria Checklist

This is the core filter applied to every stock I consider. Score out of 20.

### Income Quality

| # | Criterion | Threshold |
|---|-----------|-----------|
| 1 | Revenue Growth | ≥ 5% annual |
| 2 | Gross Margin | ≥ 40% |
| 3 | Operating Margin | ≥ 15% |
| 4 | SG&A / Gross Profit | < 30% |
| 5 | Depreciation / Gross Profit | < 10% |
| 6 | Interest Expense / Operating Income | < 15% |
| 7 | Net Margin | ≥ 20% |
| 8 | Earnings Growth | ≥ 7% annual (3yr avg) |

### Cash Flow Strength

| # | Criterion | Threshold |
|---|-----------|-----------|
| 9 | FCF Margin | ≥ 5% |
| 15 | CAPEX / Net Income | ≤ 25% |
| 16 | CAPEX / Sales | < 5% |
| 17 | CAPEX / Operating Cash Flow | < 15% |

### Balance Sheet Health

| # | Criterion | Threshold |
|---|-----------|-----------|
| 10 | Total Liabilities / Equity | < 0.8 |
| 18 | EBIT / Interest Payments | > 10x |
| 19 | Net Debt / FCF | < 4x (net cash preferred) |

### Returns on Capital

| # | Criterion | Threshold |
|---|-----------|-----------|
| 11 | ROE | ≥ 15% |
| 12 | ROA | ≥ 7% |
| 13 | ROIC | ≥ 15% |

### Valuation

| # | Criterion | Threshold |
|---|-----------|-----------|
| 14 | P/E vs 5-year Average | Current P/E < historical avg |

### Verdict

| Score | Decision |
|-------|---------|

## Part 6: Valuation — Buying at the Right Price

Even a great company is a bad investment if you overpay.

### 6.1 The Valuation Tools

**DCF (Discounted Cash Flow)**
- Best for cash-generative businesses
- Use Simply Wall St for fair value estimates
- Target: current price at a **≥ 50% discount** to DCF fair value (margin of safety)

**Multiples Valuation**
Use multiples to compare companies within the same industry. Choose the multiple that fits the business:

| Multiple | Best used for |
|----------|--------------|
| P/E | Profitable companies |
| P/B | Asset-heavy or financial companies; meaningless for services |
| P/S | Pre-profit or high-growth companies |

- Compare vs. the company's own 5-year historical average
- Compare vs. industry peers (apples to apples only — small differences in business models change these values significantly)

**P/B and ROE together:** A company with a low P/B relative to peers but a high ROE is worth investigating — it may be underpriced relative to its capital efficiency.

### 6.2 The Quality Paradox

Quality companies often trade at premium multiples. This creates a timing problem: strong ROIC and FCF alone are not enough — you need a **valuation reset** (compression, drawdown, or earnings disappointment) to get an attractive entry point.

Watch for:
- P/E compressing toward or below fair P/E (SWS estimate)
- A 30–50% discount to DCF fair value
- Analyst target upside > 20%

### 6.3 Margin of Safety

Never buy at fair value. The market is unpredictable enough that paying full price leaves no room for error. A 50% discount to intrinsic value provides the buffer.

## Part 7: Portfolio Construction

### 7.1 Concentration vs. Diworsification

**Diworsification:** buying many assets without analysis just to diversify. This contradicts the "know what you own" principle.

The right number of holdings: **10–30 well-researched positions.** More than that dilutes the quality of your analysis and returns.

### 7.2 Stock-to-Bond Ratio

There is no magic ratio. Choose based on your risk profile:

| Approach | Trade-off |
|----------|-----------|
| Fixed ratio, periodically rebalanced | Minimizes risk |
| Leave it alone | Maximizes return, more volatile |
| Adjust with market conditions | Active management, requires discipline |

### 7.3 Fast Compounders Grow Into Large Positions

A fast-growing position will naturally become your largest holding over time — even if you didn't intend it to. Monitor concentration and consider rebalancing if a single position grows beyond your comfort level.

## Part 8: Behavioral Discipline

Investing has a price — paid not in money but in **volatility, fear, doubt, uncertainty, and regret.** These are unavoidable. The question is whether you are prepared to pay them.

| Bias | What it looks like | Counter |
|------|-------------------|---------|
| **FOMO** | Chasing hot stocks, popular funds | Ask: is the thesis in the price already? |
| **Loss aversion** | Selling at the bottom | Pre-define your thesis before buying |
| **Overconfidence** | Ignoring the checklist | Run the numbers every time |
| **Herd behavior** | Buying when everyone buys | Opposite market action creates opportunity |

**When the market panics, stay calm.** Panic selling creates buying opportunities. The best assets get sold at discounts by fearful investors. This is when discipline turns into return.

---

## Part 8: DCA vs. Lump Sum

DCA (Dollar Cost Averaging) is a tool, not a strategy. Understand what it implies:

- DCA assumes the market will be lower in the near future — it's a form of partial market timing
- Doing it constantly implies you always expect a decline → if that's true, you should never invest
- **Lump sum investing outperforms DCA over time** because "time in the market beats timing the market"
- DCA is useful when you genuinely expect a near-term decline, not as a default habit

## Part 9: Sources & Research Process

For any stock analysis, use these sources in order:

1. **Simply Wall St** — valuation (DCF fair value), financial health score, peer comparison
2. **Fiscal.ai** — financial statement ratios (search via web; direct fetch blocked)
3. **SEC 10-K / Annual Reports** — raw financials, management guidance
4. **StockAnalysis.com** — ROIC, revenue, earnings growth
5. **Web search** — analyst targets, recent earnings, news

**Never use training-data financial figures** for investment decisions. Always fetch live data.

## Quick Reference — Deal-Breakers

Stop the analysis and pass if any of these are true:

- Operating margin < 5%
- Total debt/equity > 2x
- Retained earnings < 0 (unless newly profitable with clear trajectory)
- Gross margin < 40% (for software/tech)
- FCF margin < 0% for 3+ consecutive years
- CEO with no meaningful stock ownership
- Score < 10/20 on the checklist
